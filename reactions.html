Section 1 • Unsaturated Hydrocarbons

Alkenes: Addition Reactions & Alcohol Dehydration

C=C Functional Group

Alkenes contain the unsaturated carbon-to-carbon double bond (C=C). In an addition reaction, one bond of the C=C double bond breaks open and two new atoms or groups join across the two carbon atoms, forming a single saturated product (100% atom economy).

Hydrogenation, Bromination & Dehydration Core Reactions

1. Catalytic Hydrogenation (+H₂): Alkenes react with hydrogen gas over a finely divided nickel (Ni) catalyst to form saturated alkanes. In the food industry, this hardens liquid unsaturated vegetable oils into spreadable solid fats (margarine) by removing C=C kinks and increasing London dispersion forces.

2. Bromination (+Br₂): Adding orange/brown bromine solution across a C=C bond forms a colourless 1,2-dibromoalkane immediately. The volume of Br₂ required is directly proportional to the number of C=C double bonds.

3. Dehydration of Alcohols (Elimination): Passing alcohol vapour over heated aluminium oxide (Al₂O₃) catalyst removes H₂O to form an alkene. Dehydrating butan-2-ol yields a mixture of but-1-ene and but-2-ene.

Both fats and oils are triesters (triglycerides) formed from 1 molecule of glycerol (propane-1,2,3-triol) and 3 fatty acid molecules.

Section 5 • Natural Polyamides

Proteins: Peptide Links, Hydrolysis & Denaturation

–CONH– Amide Link
Condensation & Hydrolysis of Peptide Links –NH₂ + –COOH

Amino acids contain both an amino group (–NH₂) and a carboxyl group (–COOH). They join by condensation polymerisation, eliminating water to form peptide (amide) links (–CONH–).

Exam Tip: Drawing Amino Acids from a Protein Segment
When asked to draw the amino acids formed by hydrolysing a protein section, draw a vertical split line through every C(=O) — N(H) bond, then add –OH back onto the C=O carbon and –H back onto the N–H nitrogen!
Denaturation vs Hydrolysis Crucial Distinction
Feature Protein Denaturation Protein Hydrolysis
Cause Increasing temperature (heat) or changing pH. Heating under reflux with acid/alkali or enzyme digestion (+H₂O).
Bonds Broken Weak intermolecular hydrogen bonds holding the 3D coiled shape. Strong covalent peptide links (–CONH–) along the polymer chain.
Result 3D shape unfolds; enzyme active site changes shape; texture changes (e.g. cooked egg white). Polypeptide breaks down completely into individual amino acid molecules.
Section 6 • Chemistry of Fragrances

Terpenes, Isoprene Units & Terpenoid Oxidation

2-methylbuta-1,3-diene (C₅H₈)
The Isoprene Building Block (C₅H₈) 2-methylbuta-1,3-diene

Terpenes are unsaturated hydrocarbons found in essential oils, built by joining together units of isoprene (systematic name: 2-methylbuta-1,3-diene, molecular formula C₅H₈).

Counting Isoprene Units & Oxidation Terpene → Terpenoid

Counting Rule: Because every isoprene unit contains exactly 5 carbon atoms, divide the total number of carbons in the terpene skeleton by 5:

  • Limonene / Menthol (C₁₀): 10 ÷ 5 = 2 isoprene units
  • Farnesene / Bisabolene (C₁₅): 15 ÷ 5 = 3 isoprene units
  • Squalene (C₃₀H₅₀): 30 ÷ 5 = 6 isoprene units
  • β-Carotene (C₄₀H₅₆): 40 ÷ 5 = 8 isoprene units

Oxidation of Terpenes: Terpenes can be oxidised within plants (or on exposure to light and air) to produce oxygenated terpenoids (alcohols, aldehydes, ketones) responsible for distinctive spice and herb fragrances. Essential oils are stored in dark brown glass bottles to prevent UV-accelerated oxidation from spoiling their aroma.
